PMM Context

The full arc: position, launch, stall, reposition

Orthians Polycraft is a D2C memory-foam ergonomics brand I co-founded. I owned go-to-market from day zero — buyer research, competitive positioning, messaging, pricing, and channel strategy. In-house manufacturing was the structural advantage that made the whole position possible.

The category had players, but the field split in two: cheap-and-flimsy, or expensive-and-medical. Nobody was competing on genuine quality at the mainstream price. Our thesis: enter at the market leader's exact price point with a demonstrably better product, funded by manufacturing cost savings the competition didn't have.

Positioning thesis — better product, same price. Higher foam density and better fabric at the leader's price point. The savings from in-house manufacturing went into the product, not the margin. Price was the positioning: parity on cost, superiority on quality.

This case study has two chapters. Chapter one: the buyer, positioning, messaging, and go-to-market that took Orthians from idea to launch. Chapter two: what happened when ratings and returns — not the product — capped acquisition, and the repositioning around trust that restarted growth. Chapter two is where I learned the most.

Chapter one · the buyer

Who we were selling to

Everything downstream — the position, the message, the price, the channel — had to answer one specific buyer. I built the profile from 15+ interviews across returners, keepers, and long-term users, plus review mining on our listings and competitors'.

Who
Tier-1 urban professionals, 20–35. Desk workers and long commuters with rising disposable income and growing back and neck pain from long hours sitting.
How they buy
Shop online, read reviews before committing, willing to pay for quality — but skeptical, because they've bought foam that flattened in months before.
Evaluates on
Proof it lasts · ratings and reviews · a way not to get burned (returns, warranty) · whether it looks worth the price.
Jobs to be done
Functional

End the back and neck pain from long hours at a desk or in the car — with something that keeps working, not another pillow I replace in three months.

Emotional

Feel sure I'm not throwing ₹2,000 at a gimmick that lets me down.

Social / identity

Own something that looks premium on my desk — not a clinical medical device.

The objections blocking the sale

Four doubts came up again and again. Naming them is the job — because the position and the message exist to answer them, and chapter two is what happened when one went unanswered.

#The objection in the buyer's words
1"It'll flatten in a few months like every other foam I've tried." (durability doubt)
2"Why ₹2,000 when there are ₹800 options?" (price vs value)
3"What if it doesn't work for my body?" (purchase risk)
4"It looks medical — not something I want on show." (perception)
Chapter one · positioning & messaging

The position, and the message that carried it

Positioning statement

For desk-bound urban professionals burned by ergonomic products that flatten in months, Orthians is the memory-foam ergonomics brand that delivers durable, genuine pain relief at the mainstream price — unlike budget brands that cut corners or medical brands that overcharge — because in-house manufacturing lets us put better materials into the product, not into margin.

Chapter one · go-to-market

Pricing and channel to reach the buyer

With the buyer and the position set, two calls decided how the message reached the market — and one of them planted the problem chapter two had to solve.

Pricing as positioning
A pricing analysis put us deliberately in line with the market leader — not below it. In-house manufacturing kept COGS near ₹550 on a ₹2,000 SKU (roughly 60% gross margin vs an industry average of 35–40%), so matching the leader's price still left room to fund the trust-building a challenger needs. Price wasn't a markup on cost — it was a signal: same price as the leader, a better product. The tradeoff: in-house meant heavier fixed costs and capital tied up in capability instead of inventory.
Channel: where the buyer already shops
Amazon and Flipkart first, where category buyers search and where reviews compound into trust. Our own site as the margin driver once trust was established. No offline retail: lower fixed cost, faster feedback loops, and the category already had proven online purchase behaviour. The tradeoff: marketplace dependence made ratings and reviews our top-of-funnel — and that dependence is exactly where chapter two begins.
The seed of the problem: choosing marketplaces meant a marketing signal we didn't fully control — the star rating — became the gate on every new sale. Great when it works for you. In chapter two, it worked against us.
Chapter two · the trust wall

When trust signals capped growth

Months into launch, the model started leaking. Returns were high. Average ratings sat below 4.0. Retention was weak. And new purchases flattened — because the GTM bet had made marketplace trust signals our front door. A sub-4.0 rating suppresses conversion and organic rank, and every return burns reverse-logistics cost and usually leaves a bad review behind.

The uncomfortable part: the product tested well in hand. So this wasn't a product problem — it was a positioning and expectations problem. What buyers expected at the point of purchase wasn't aligned with how, and when, the product actually delivered its value.

Problem statement: high returns and weak retention were dragging average ratings below 4.0, which capped new-user acquisition. Diagnose why buyers were rejecting a product that beat the market — and fix it without changing the price or the product line.
Why returns hurt twice: the 60% gross margin from chapter one was the plan's engine. Every avoidable return handed that margin to reverse logistics, and often traded it for a one-star review on top — hitting both the P&L and the top of the funnel at once.
Diagnosis · what the buyer research surfaced

The same 15+ interviews and review mining that built the buyer profile now explained the leak. Sampling skewed toward reachable users, so I treated the findings as directional — but the pattern was consistent enough to bet on.

Insight 1 — Value arrives at day 20–25. The return window closed at day 7.
Users who stayed consistent for 20–25 days reported real pain reduction. But the 7-day return window forced a keep-or-return verdict in week one, before the product had any chance to prove itself. Buyers were returning defensively — "while I still can." The purchase experience had set the wrong timeline; the deadline was manufacturing returns, not the product. This was objection #3 going unanswered.
Insight 2 — At ₹2,000, the unboxing is part of the pitch
Buyers spending ₹2,000 expected the object itself to feel worth it. Ergonomic products look unfamiliar, and when the first impression read "medical" instead of "premium," doubt set in before the foam got a chance. Perceived quality is a marketing surface — and ours was quietly underselling a product that deserved better. Objection #4, live in the box.
Insight 3 — Durability was our strongest unclaimed asset
Market-standard foam loses shape in 3–4 months. Long-term users confirmed ours held for 12–14 months — 3–4× the category norm, a verified outcome from real usage, not a spec sheet. We had a genuine advantage buyers couldn't see at purchase, and we were claiming it nowhere. An unmade claim is a wasted one — and the answer to objection #1.
Chapter two · the repositioning

Three trust bets, and what moved

The fix wasn't a new product — it was aligning the promise, the claims, and the buying experience with how the product actually delivered value. Three bets, each closing one of the open objections.

The counterintuitive bet: extending the return window 4× would reduce returns, not increase them. If value lands at day 20–25, a 7-day deadline guarantees defensive exits. Give the product time to prove itself and those exits disappear. The risk was real — more time could mean more returns if the read was wrong — so return rate became the guardrail metric, watched weekly.

The old policy asked buyers for a verdict two weeks before the product could deliver one.
Bet 1 — Return window 7 → 30 days (answers "what if it doesn't work for me?")
Aligned the buying experience to the value curve. Buyers now judge the product after feeling the benefit, not before. This was an expectation-setting decision about when users experience value — not a CX generosity gesture.
Bet 2 — 1-year warranty (answers "it'll flatten in months")
12–14 months of verified shape retention gave us the confidence to back it with a 1-year warranty — and it planted a claim competitors working with 3–4 month foam could not safely match. The product's real edge, finally made legible to the buyer at the point of purchase.
Bet 3 — 30-day replacement (answers "it looks clinical / arrived wrong")
A damaged or underwhelming first impression now had an instant resolution path that kept the user in the product instead of exiting to a refund. Replacements preserve the relationship; refunds end it — and this de-risked the first-impression problem directly.

Guardrails I watched
Return rate under the longer window
The core risk of the bet. If returns had risen instead of fallen, the 30-day window would have been rolled back. Reviewed weekly.
Warranty claim rate vs margin
A 1-year promise carries cost exposure. Claims had to stay small enough that the trust upside outweighed the replacement cost — which the 12–14 month durability data supported.
Rating trajectory, not just level
Direction mattered more than the snapshot. A rating climbing toward 4.0 with growing review volume is a recovering brand. A flat 3.9 would have meant the fix wasn't compounding.
Reflection

What this taught me

Two chapters, two sets of lessons — most of them about marketing, not manufacturing.

From the positioning
Your GTM choice picks your master metric
Choosing marketplaces made ratings our oxygen — a marketing signal that gates conversion and rank. I'd make the same call again, but I'd instrument trust metrics from day one instead of discovering their weight after launch.
Pricing is a positioning decision, not a cost calculation
The 60% margin wasn't just profit headroom. It was what let us match the leader's price, fund trust-building, and later underwrite a 1-year warranty. Pricing, manufacturing, and messaging were one decision wearing three hats.
From the turnaround
Returns and ratings are marketing problems as often as product ones
The foam already beat the market. What was broken was the gap between the expectations we set at purchase and when value actually landed. Fixing the promise and the message — not the product — restarted growth.
A claim you can defend beats a feature you can't communicate
Durability stayed invisible until the warranty turned it into a claim rivals couldn't safely match. Positioning is the work of making your real advantage legible to the buyer at the moment of decision.
Trust metrics are top-of-funnel metrics
On marketplaces, the rating gates conversion and organic rank. Moving it toward 4.0 restarted new-purchase growth without a rupee of extra ad spend.
Looking forward
What I'd do differently
Compress the path to first paying users. Twenty manual sales over WhatsApp and Instagram DMs before full listings would have surfaced the day-20–25 value curve before launch instead of after it — so the 30-day window and the durability claim could have been the launch positioning, not the fix.
What I'd do next
Shift review requests from the standard day 3–5 to day 25+, after value lands, so reviews capture the real experience. Add day 7, 14, and 21 check-ins to keep users consistent through the value curve. And push shape retention beyond today's 12–14 months to widen the durability moat — then let the warranty claim follow it.
